My research goal is to answer one question, stated provisionally in the proposal: "Can a wrist-worn ESP32-S3 device with a single PDM MEMS microphone, acting as the live-capture and real-time-display front end of a preset-driven singing-voice analyzer, estimate singing f0 within **±20 cents median absolute error (≥90 % RPA @ 50 cents)** on the acoustic path and **≤5 cents vs Praat** on the digital-injection path, render a **≥30 Hz spectrogram (50 Hz for the live-singing preset) with ≤80 ms acoustic-to-photon latency**, and sustain **≥3 h** of continuous analysis on its own battery — with all real-time DSP on-device and the host used only for offline analysis of recorded takes?" (proposal §1, prov.). I am an embedded software engineer whose professional foundation is in real-time, safety-critical systems (automotive / ADAS): hard deadlines, deterministic scheduling, resource-bounded compute. I want to bring that discipline to a measurement problem that sits between acoustics, signal processing and human-centred design: whether a commodity smartwatch can give a singer metrologically honest feedback in real time.

To investigate this, I designed and am documenting *Super Spectral*, a companion system. A LilyGO T-Watch S3 — one ESP32-S3, one Knowles PDM microphone, a 240×240 display — runs the entire live path on-device: PDM capture, windowed FFT with stated normalization, time-domain pitch estimation, band-energy and singer's-formant readouts, and a hardware-scrolled spectrogram, on ESP-IDF with an explicitly pinned and reproducible environment. A Linux host performs the offline science on recorded takes — Praat-grade formants, long-term average spectra, spectral-tilt measures — under a deliberately separate licence so it may use Praat directly. The central contribution is methodological as much as technical: every number is reported on two paths, a digital-injection path that measures the algorithm against Praat golden files with a pinned manifest, and an acoustic path through a calibrated mouth simulator that measures the whole chain — case, port, microphone, clock and estimator — so that wearable claims and algorithm claims can never be confused.

This is not a concept sketch. The project carries a formal research question, five objectives, and a three-phase validation plan: component characterization of the microphone path in situ through the watch case; a **108-trial bench matrix** (preset × sound level × distance × arm angle, provisional pending a power analysis) scored with the MIREX/`mir_eval` conventions and Bland–Altman agreement statistics; and in-use validation with singers wearing the device alongside a reference microphone. Architectural choices are recorded as decision records, the on-flash record formats are versioned binary contracts, the toolchain is pinned to the commit and reproduced in CI, and the whole repository — documentation, firmware, golden files, data manifests — is open so that a third party can rebuild the device and rerun the experiments within the stated tolerances. I am currently at the documentation-and-environment stage: the bibliography, decision backlog and validation plan exist; the first firmware milestone is gated on them.
